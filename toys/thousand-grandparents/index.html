Where it collapses

The row marked both is where the argument lands. Slaveholders and enslaved people were not two sealed populations that happened to share a continent — they shared households, and the genetic record of that is not ambiguous. So the honest question is never which of the two am I descended from. Push back far enough and the arithmetic stops offering that choice.

Doubling beats any population that grows slower than doubling — and every real one does. Once your slots pass the number of people alive, you are no longer drawing some ancestors from that population. You are drawing nearly all of them, repeatedly. This is Chang's result CHANG: in a population of size n, everyone shares a common ancestor about log2(n) generations back, and not much further back every person alive is an ancestor of everyone or of no one.

What the model does, and where it lies

Two different kinds of number are on this page, and mixing them up is how this argument usually goes wrong.

Counting results — the enslaved-ancestor odds for a Black line, the slaveholding-ancestor odds for a white Southern line, the crossing year. These fall out of arithmetic on census counts: P(at least one) = 1 − (1 − p)n over n independent lines. The independence assumption is the weak joint — real trees cluster by county, church and class, which pushes the true number down from what's shown.

Measured results — every number that crosses the color line. These are not derived; they are read off genotype data from ~160,000 people BRYC. African Americans carry 24.0% European ancestry on average, overwhelmingly through male lines during slavery. 3.5% of self-identified European Americans carry ≥1% African ancestry — but 13% in South Carolina and 12% in Louisiana.

Those cross-line figures carry a ≥ because they are detection floors, not estimates. Bryc's threshold is 1% of the genome. A single ancestor g generations back contributes 2−g on average — about 0.4% at eight generations, 0.1% at ten. Anyone whose only enslaved ancestor stands further back than roughly seven generations is invisible to the measurement and is counted as a negative. The true share is higher by an unknown amount, and this page will not guess at it.

Three things the model gets wrong on purpose, in the direction of understatement: generations are fixed at 28 years when the real interval drifts; pedigree collapse is handled by the occupancy model rather than measured; and lines are treated as exchangeable within a region when in fact slaveholding concentrated in exactly the families most likely to leave many descendants.
